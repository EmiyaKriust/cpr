[AHA2025 Guidelines·Overview] The 2025 AHA Adult Basic Life Support Guidelines cover cardiac arrest recognition, emergency response activation, high-quality CPR, AED use, and foreign-body airway obstruction management.
[AHA2025 Guidelines·Overview] Early high-quality CPR and prompt defibrillation are the most important interventions for improved outcomes in adult cardiac arrest.
[AHA2025 Guidelines·Overview] Approximately 356,000 adults are treated by EMS for OHCA annually in the US; survival to hospital discharge is about 10.5%, with favorable neurological outcome in 8.2%.
[AHA2025 Guidelines·Overview] AHA 2030 Impact Goals: increase bystander CPR rates to above 50% and increase AED application before EMS arrival to above 20%.
[Adult BLS·Cardiac Arrest Recognition] Lay rescuer: If an adult is unconscious/unresponsive with absent or abnormal breathing (only gasping), assume cardiac arrest.
[Adult BLS·Cardiac Arrest Recognition] Healthcare professional: Check pulse for no more than 10 seconds; if no definite pulse is felt, assume cardiac arrest.
[Adult BLS·Cardiac Arrest Recognition] Agonal breathing (slow, irregular gasping) is present in 40-60% of OHCA cases and is a common reason lay rescuers misdiagnose cardiac arrest.
[Adult BLS·Cardiac Arrest Recognition] The risk of harm from performing chest compressions on an unconscious person not in cardiac arrest is very low; the benefit of CPR far outweighs the risk.
[Adult BLS·Initiation of Resuscitation] All lay rescuers should provide chest compressions for adults with presumed cardiac arrest.
[Adult BLS·Initiation of Resuscitation] A lone responder should activate the emergency response system first, then immediately begin CPR starting with chest compressions.
[Adult BLS·Initiation of Resuscitation] A lone rescuer with a mobile phone should use speaker mode to activate emergency response while simultaneously starting CPR.
[Adult BLS·Initiation of Resuscitation] Trained lay rescuers should provide both chest compressions and ventilations for adult cardiac arrest.
[Adult BLS·Initiation of Resuscitation] It is reasonable to use PPE during CPR; however, donning PPE should not significantly delay CPR initiation.
[Adult BLS·Initiation of Resuscitation] A lone healthcare professional should start with chest compressions rather than ventilation (C-A-B sequence).
[Adult BLS·Initiation of Resuscitation] Healthcare professionals should perform chest compressions and ventilation for all adult cardiac arrest patients regardless of cardiac or noncardiac cause.
[Adult BLS·Airway Management] When no cervical spine injury is suspected, use the head tilt-chin lift maneuver to open the airway.
[Adult BLS·Airway Management] With known or suspected basilar skull fracture or severe coagulopathy, an oropharyngeal airway is preferred over a nasopharyngeal airway.
[Adult BLS·Airway Management] Oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway adjuncts may be reasonable in unconscious patients with no cough or gag reflex.
[Adult BLS·Airway Management] Routine use of cricoid pressure during adult cardiac arrest is not recommended.
[Adult BLS·Trauma Airway] In head/neck trauma, trained rescuers should use the jaw thrust without head extension to open the airway.
[Adult BLS·Trauma Airway] If jaw thrust and airway adjunct fail, trained rescuers should use head tilt-chin lift - airway patency takes priority over spinal injury risk.
[Adult BLS·Trauma Airway] Lay rescuers should NOT use rigid cervical collars for spinal motion restriction; these devices may impair airway patency and ventilation.
[Adult BLS·Compression Position] Perform chest compressions with the patient's torso at approximately the level of the rescuer's knees.
[Adult BLS·Compression Position] Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest (lower half to lower third of sternum), other hand on top, hands overlapped.
[Adult BLS·Compression Position] Resuscitation should generally be conducted where the patient is found, as long as high-quality CPR can be administered safely and effectively.
[Adult BLS·Compression Position] When feasible without delaying compressions, perform CPR on a firm surface with the patient in supine position.
[Adult BLS·Compression Position] Rescuers may consider placing their dominant hand on the sternum when performing chest compressions.
[Adult BLS·Compression Position] For prone-position cardiac arrest, turn the patient supine if safely possible; if not, prone CPR may be considered.
[Adult BLS·Chest Compression Fraction] Preshock and postshock pauses in chest compressions should be as short as possible.
[Adult BLS·Chest Compression Fraction] During rhythm checks, pulse check must not exceed 10 seconds; if no definite pulse, resume compressions immediately.
[Adult BLS·Chest Compression Fraction] Immediately resume chest compressions after shock rather than pause for postshock rhythm check.
[Adult BLS·Chest Compression Fraction] When 2 or more rescuers are available, switch the chest compressor approximately every 2 minutes (or after about 5 cycles of 30:2).
[Adult BLS·Chest Compression Fraction] Without an advanced airway, pause compressions to deliver 2 breaths, each given over 1 second.
[Adult BLS·Chest Compression Fraction] Maintain a chest compression fraction (CCF) of at least 60% during adult cardiac arrest resuscitation.
[Adult BLS·Compression Depth and Rate] Perform chest compressions to a depth of at least 5 cm (2 inches), avoiding excessive depth greater than 6 cm (2.4 inches).
[Adult BLS·Compression Depth and Rate] Real-time audiovisual feedback devices are reasonable for optimizing CPR performance and improving ROSC and survival.
[Adult BLS·Compression Depth and Rate] Perform chest compressions at a rate of 100 to 120 per minute.
[Adult BLS·Compression Depth and Rate] Allow complete chest wall recoil between compressions; do not lean on the chest.
[Adult BLS·Compression Depth and Rate] It is reasonable for compression and recoil/relaxation times to be approximately equal (50% duty cycle).
[Adult BLS·Ventilation Fundamentals] Without an advanced airway, deliver breaths by mouth-to-mouth, mouth-to-mask, or bag-mask ventilation.
[Adult BLS·Ventilation Fundamentals] Give enough tidal volume to produce visible chest rise while avoiding hypoventilation and hyperventilation.
[Adult BLS·Ventilation Fundamentals] Bag-mask ventilation is most effective with 2 trained rescuers: one opens airway and seals mask with both hands, the other squeezes the bag.
[Adult BLS·Ventilation Fundamentals] Each breath should be delivered over 1 second to keep compression pauses as short as possible.
[Adult BLS·Ventilation Fundamentals] Hypoventilation (too few breaths or too little volume) and hyperventilation (too many breaths or too large volume) are both harmful.
[Adult BLS·Ventilation Fundamentals] A tidal volume of approximately 362-406 mL (about 5-6 mL/kg) is needed to achieve visible chest rise.
[Adult BLS·Special Ventilation] Mouth-to-nose ventilation may be used if ventilation through the mouth is impossible.
[Adult BLS·Special Ventilation] For patients with a tracheal stoma, mouth-to-stoma or face mask-to-stoma ventilation may be reasonable.
[Adult BLS·Respiratory Arrest] For adults with a pulse but no effective breathing, provide ventilations.
[Adult BLS·Respiratory Arrest] For respiratory arrest, give 1 ventilation every 6 seconds (10 breaths per minute), each producing visible chest rise.
[Adult BLS·Compression-to-Ventilation Ratio] Perform CPR with cycles of 30 compressions followed by 2 breaths before placement of an advanced airway.
[Adult BLS·Compression-to-Ventilation Ratio] The 30:2 CPR ratio is associated with improved ROSC, survival to admission, and favorable neurological outcome compared to continuous compressions with asynchronous ventilations.
[Adult BLS·Compression-to-Ventilation Ratio] Continuous chest compressions with asynchronous breaths do not allow the rescuer to observe chest rise to monitor ventilation adequacy.
[Adult BLS·Defibrillation] Place defibrillation pads on exposed chest in anterolateral or anteroposterior position; electrode diameter should be greater than 8 cm.
[Adult BLS·Defibrillation] When placing defibrillation pads, adjusting the bra rather than removing it may be reasonable.
[Adult BLS·Defibrillation] Female cardiac arrest patients have significantly lower rates of AED use; even pads placed directly over bra underwire achieved 100% first-shock success with no adverse events.
[Adult BLS·Defibrillation] Continue CPR until a defibrillator or AED is applied and ready.
[Adult BLS·Defibrillation] In unmonitored cardiac arrest, provide a brief period of CPR while the defibrillator is being obtained and readied.
[Adult BLS·Defibrillation] For witnessed or monitored VF/pulseless VT with a defibrillator immediately available, perform immediate defibrillation.
[Adult BLS·Defibrillation] Every minute of delay to first shock is associated with a 6% decrease in probability of survival to hospital discharge.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] For severe foreign-body airway obstruction: perform repeated cycles of 5 back blows followed by 5 abdominal thrusts until the object is expelled or the person becomes unresponsive.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] Activate the emergency response system immediately for severe FBAO.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] For mild FBAO: allow the person to clear the airway by coughing while observing for signs of severe obstruction.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] If a person with severe FBAO becomes unresponsive, start CPR beginning with chest compressions and activate emergency response.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] During CPR for FBAO, remove any visible foreign body when opening the airway to provide breaths.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] Do NOT perform blind finger sweeps - these may worsen the airway obstruction.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] If unable to encircle the patient's abdomen (obesity, wheelchair) or in late-stage pregnancy, use cycles of 5 back blows followed by 5 chest thrusts.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] Back blows are associated with higher rates of FBAO relief and fewer injuries compared to abdominal thrusts; abdominal thrusts may cause aortic dissection and gastric rupture.
[Adult BLS·FBAO] The effectiveness and safety of suction-based airway clearance devices have not been established; evidence is insufficient.
[Adult BLS·Obesity] CPR for adults with obesity should use the same techniques as for non-obese patients.
[Adult BLS·Obesity] Obesity increases chest wall thickness, requiring greater force to achieve the recommended compression depth.
[Adult BLS·Obesity] When moving obese patients to a firm surface, carefully weigh the risk of delays in initiating chest compressions.
[Adult BLS·Obesity] A partial backboard (CPR board) does not improve chest compression adequacy compared to a hospital mattress alone for obese patients.
[Adult BLS·Alternative CPR Techniques] Mechanical CPR devices may be considered in specific settings (transport, rescuer safety concerns) but interruptions during device deployment and removal must be strictly limited.
[Adult BLS·Alternative CPR Techniques] Routine use of mechanical CPR devices is NOT recommended for adult cardiac arrest - large RCTs show no survival benefit.
[Adult BLS·Alternative CPR Techniques] For single rescuers in confined spaces (avalanche, small spaces), over-the-head CPR (OTH-CPR) may be considered - kneel with patient's head between knees and compress the sternum.
[Adult BLS·Alternative CPR Techniques] OTH-CPR shows no difference or improvement in compression depth, rate, and ventilation quality compared to lateral CPR.
[Adult BLS·Alternative CPR Techniques] The effectiveness of heel/foot compressions is not well established; insufficient evidence for recommendation.
[Adult BLS·Rescuer Safety] Use of PPE during CPR is reasonable; CPR is considered an aerosol-generating procedure. PPE may increase rescuer fatigue but does not affect CPR quality or survival.
[Adult BLS·Key Data] Only 47.7% of OHCA patients receive bystander CPR; only 7.9% have an AED applied by a bystander.
[Adult BLS·Key Data] When bed height exceeds the rescuer's knee level by more than 20 cm, average compression depth is significantly reduced.
[Adult BLS·Key Data] Compression depth begins to decrease after 90-120 seconds of CPR, supporting the recommendation to rotate compressors every 2 minutes.
[Summary] The 2025 AHA Adult BLS Guidelines emphasize evidence-based, precision resuscitation across the complete Chain of Survival from recognition to defibrillation.
